Reference analysis and implementation requirements

Modernist Cuisine Recipe Web Design Specification

Recreate the information architecture and editorial character of the supplied Caramelized Carrot Soup recipe card as a responsive recipe page. The defining feature is a five-column preparation matrix: ingredient quantities sit beside the procedure that consumes them. Build around that relationship rather than treating ingredients and instructions as unrelated sections.

This demonstrates a many-to-many layout: one ingredient can accompany several steps, and several ingredients can accompany several steps.

2. Page information architecture

Use this reading order in both the page and its underlying content:

  1. Recipe identity: one title and optional source attribution.
  2. Recipe facts: yield, time estimate, storage, difficulty, equipment and component requirements, in that order.
  3. Editorial introduction: ingredient selection, technique rationale, alternatives, and serving context. A short dish story may be included here when authored for the recipe; it is not a separate module visible in the reference.
  4. Preparation matrix: ordered ingredient/procedure groups, with steps numbered continuously across groups.
  5. Supporting notes: linked technique guidance, component references, and attribution where applicable.

Keep the matrix visible immediately after the introduction. Do not insert a large photograph, expanded narrative, newsletter panel, ratings panel, or unrelated content between these sections. Optional print and yield controls belong in a quiet utility row outside the reference composition.

Recipe requirements can contain several equipment items and component recipes. Represent each separately in the data even when the page displays them as a single sentence. Omit absent optional metadata rows cleanly. Do not invent storage durations, timing, difficulty, or equipment.

5. Preparation matrix behavior

Group is the primary layout unit

Each group contains an ingredient list and a contiguous range of steps. Ingredients share the first four columns; the procedure occupies the fifth. A rule separates groups, not every ingredient or every step. Start the ingredients and the first step at the same top edge. Let the longer side determine group height; blank space on the shorter side is intentional.

An ingredient line must never be horizontally aligned with a later step in a way that implies an ingredient-to-step mapping the recipe does not actually specify. Keep exact associations at group level unless the authored recipe includes explicit per-step ingredient references.

Quantity presentation

  • Keep ingredient preparation in the name or a secondary line: for example, peeled, drained, finely chopped. Component references use a smaller separate line.
  • Store measured quantities as numbers plus units. Format with nonbreaking spaces, for example 250 g; keep short fractions and units together.
  • Weight is the principal formulation measure. Volume is an alternative approximation or practical measure, not an additional quantity to add.
  • Allow mass, count, and volume representations to coexist without inferring conversions. Ingredient density is needed for mass-to-volume conversion.
  • “To taste,” “as needed,” and unspecified quantities are qualitative values. Leave their scaling percentage blank. Do not display zero or manufacture a number.
  • When a quantity is unavailable, show a typographic dash or an empty cell with an accessible “not specified” label, consistently. Do not collapse a column because one ingredient lacks that measure.

Scaling semantics

Store an explicit reference ingredient ID or a defined reference mass. For mass-based formulations, percentage = ingredient mass / reference mass × 100. Mark the base ingredient as 100%; show values above 100% normally. Add a quiet explanatory note or accessible help text identifying the actual base. Do not relabel the column “% of total.”

If a recipe has separately formulated components, each component may require its own base. In that case, identify each base in that component’s heading or note. A recipe without a meaningful formulation base should omit the Scaling column for the entire recipe, using a declared four-column variant rather than invented percentages.

Procedure and notes

Number steps globally from 1 to N, without restarting at group boundaries. Give every step a stable anchor ID. Conditions, temperatures, durations, completion cues, and exceptions belong in the relevant step. Optional steps should be stated as optional in their text. Linked component recipes should resolve to web destinations; original book page references can remain in source attribution when needed.

Use HTML lists and styled numbers rather than Unicode circled-number characters: the latter change appearance by font and become inconsistent with longer sequences. Reserve room for two- and three-digit step numbers.

On a phone, group structure becomes: thin rule → ingredient name and labeled measures, repeated for all ingredients in the group → ordered procedure steps → next group rule. Do not gather every ingredient at the top and every instruction at the bottom. Preserve the group relationships through the transformation.

Use 20 px side padding on small screens, 24 px on medium screens, and 32 px on wide screens. Recipe text remains at least 16 px. No default page-wide horizontal scrolling. At intermediate sizes, the quantities area may use a compact internal table if it fits without horizontal scrolling.

7. Semantic and accessible implementation

Use a recipe <article> containing one <h1>, a <dl> for recipe facts, a prose introduction, and a labeled preparation section. The simplest fully responsive architecture uses one semantic <section> per preparation group. Each group contains ingredient entries with nested measure definition lists and an ordered list of steps. A shared CSS grid supplies the desktop columns. The mobile layout uses the same content nodes.

Give each group an accessible name such as “Preparation group 3, steps 3 to 7.” Use an ordered list with the appropriate start value in each group. Desktop column labels are visible; individual measures retain programmatic labels through <dt> elements, visually hidden where the shared column header is sufficient. Hide decorative duplicate numbering from assistive technology while keeping native ordered-list semantics.

A native five-column table is an acceptable desktop alternative: use <colgroup>, <th scope="col">, one <tbody> per group, and a procedure cell spanning that group’s ingredient rows. Do not fabricate empty ingredients to align individual steps. If the mobile implementation uses a separate representation, derive both from the same data and ensure only the active view is in the accessibility tree. The one-DOM group-based approach is preferable for responsive reading order.

All links and controls need visible keyboard focus. Essential text must meet normal-text contrast of at least 4.5:1 against its background. The warm rules are secondary grouping cues; semantic group labels provide the relationship independently of color. Temperature and measurement symbols need readable text; do not encode quantities as images.

Quantities may be nullable. A qualitative ingredient uses quantityText and scaleMode: "qualitative". Distinguish linear, fixed, and qualitative quantities. Render recipe content as text or sanitized structured rich text. Avoid injecting arbitrary HTML from recipe data. Authored step references should point to stable ingredient IDs rather than matching ingredient names, since the same ingredient may appear in several groups.

10. Optional web interactions

These are additions to the photographed layout and should remain visually secondary.

  • Yield adjustment: multiply explicitly scalable ingredient quantities by requested yield divided by original yield. Keep formulation percentages unchanged. Keep qualitative measures unchanged. Do not automatically multiply cooking temperatures, durations, or storage notes.
  • Linked measures: where weight and volume are alternative representations of one ingredient quantity, scale both representations consistently. Do not double-count them. Use authored precision: retain 0.1 g resolution for small additives when necessary rather than rounding all weights to whole grams.
  • Embedded quantities: structured references in instruction text can update with the ingredient quantities. Literal quantities in plain text require author review; do not silently leave conflicting amounts in the procedure.
  • Capacity notes: scaling changes yield but may require different equipment or batches. Preserve any authored equipment-capacity constraints and explain them next to the scaling control.
  • Completion: optional checkboxes beside steps are separate controls with descriptive accessible names. Marking a step complete does not hide it or gray essential text below readable contrast.
  • Print: remove interactive controls; print the recipe title, metadata, introduction, full matrix, and notes.

11. Print behavior

Target both A4 and US Letter with approximately 12–15 mm margins. Use the five-column composition in print, even if the browser viewport currently shows the mobile layout. Provide a print override that restores the matrix header, column grid, and introduction columns. Aim for 10–11 pt recipe body text, 16–18 pt title, and 8–9 pt labels.

Avoid a break inside a preparation group when it fits on a page. Allow unusually long groups to split rather than overflow the page. Keep the title with its metadata and the matrix header with at least the first group. If using native tables, repeat the column header on later pages where supported. Do not force every recipe onto one page by reducing its text to unreadable sizes. Verify pagination separately; screen layout is not proof of print layout.

12. Acceptance criteria

  1. The page reads title → facts → introduction → grouped matrix → notes, with no unrelated interruption.
  2. At 1280 px viewport width, the matrix shows five columns in approximately 18:9:14:10:49 proportions.
  3. Fact labels share one left edge; fact values share a second left edge.
  4. Introduction copy flows through two columns on desktop and one on smaller screens, without hand-inserted line breaks.
  5. Each matrix group has a full-width thin warm rule, with no vertical rules, zebra striping, shadows, or enclosing cards.
  6. One ingredient with several steps and several ingredients with one step both render without fake rows.
  7. The next group begins below the taller side of the previous group. Procedure numbers remain continuous and wrapped text hangs beneath the text start.
  8. Long ingredient names and component references wrap naturally without touching neighboring columns.
  9. At 375 px and 320 px widths, ingredients precede their associated steps in every group; all quantities retain visible labels; the page does not scroll horizontally.
  10. At 200% text zoom, title, facts, quantities, and procedure remain available without clipping or overlap.
  11. A nominated base ingredient displays 100%; a larger ingredient can display more than 100%; “to taste” has no invented percentage.
  12. If yield scaling exists, halving yield halves linear weights and volumes while leaving temperatures, times, percentages, and qualitative measures unchanged.
  13. A screen reader encounters the metadata labels, group identities, quantity labels, and ordered steps in a useful cooking sequence.
  14. Print output includes all recipe content with sensible group breaks and readable type on A4 and Letter.

13. Implementation handoff

Build an editorial recipe page with white background, uppercase sans-serif title and labels, italic serif facts, two-column introductory prose, and a grouped five-column matrix. Use group-level ingredient/procedure relationships as the primary data structure. Preserve weight, volume, and reference-ingredient scaling as distinct fields. Use thin muted salmon group dividers and globally numbered steps. On narrow screens, stack each group’s ingredients above that group’s steps. Implement from structured recipe data; validate long content, qualitative quantities, scaling values above 100%, mobile reading order, keyboard access, text zoom, and print pagination.
